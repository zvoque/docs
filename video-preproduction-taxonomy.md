# Master Video Pre-Production Taxonomy

> Part of a frontier/craft reference set for steering AI video tools, editors, and creators past the default. Default reach: skip straight to generating clips with no concept, no script, no shot plan — and get a pile of pretty disconnected gens. Use the **trap** on each entry to avoid the cliché.

A working reference for everything *before* a frame is shot, generated, or coded — the named artifacts and structures that turn a brief into a plan. Covers: **concept & ideation**, **brief & treatment**, **story structures**, **advertising & marketing structures**, **scriptwriting for video**, **voiceover & timing math**, **storyboards**, **shot lists**, and **on-screen text & caption copy**. Each entry: what it is, when to reach for it, and the trap to avoid.

This is the *plan* layer of a video reference set. Its siblings cover what happens after: [`video-editing-taxonomy.md`](video-editing-taxonomy.md) (the cut), [`motion-graphics-taxonomy.md`](motion-graphics-taxonomy.md) (the visual language & camera prompts), [`ai-video-generation-playbook.md`](ai-video-generation-playbook.md) (which model to generate from), [`video-audio-taxonomy.md`](video-audio-taxonomy.md) (VO, music, sound), and [`code-rendered-video-playbook.md`](code-rendered-video-playbook.md) (programmatic/Hyperframes video). This doc is the one that comes *first* — a treatment, a script, a shot list — so the others have something to execute against.

Compiled June 2026. Trend-sensitive entries (sound-off social, current copy practice) are dated; the structural canon (three-act, AIDA, the hero's journey) is timeless and flagged as such.

---

## How to use this doc

- **Plan before you generate.** The reason most AI video looks like "a pile of clips" is that there was no treatment, no script, no shot list — generation replaced planning instead of executing it. This doc is the layer that prevents that.
- **Concept → structure → script → board → shot list is a stack.** Lock the single idea first, then the structure that carries it, then the words, then the frames, then the per-shot specs. Each layer constrains the next; skipping one shows up two layers down.
- **One idea, single-minded.** A brief, a logline, or a proposition that's secretly two ideas joined by "and" scatters every execution below it. The discipline is subtraction.
- **Write for the ear and the muted eye at once.** Modern video must survive sound-off (most social viewing is muted) *and* survive eyes-away (the radio test). On-screen text carries the meaning; audio enhances it.
- **Feeding this to an agent?** This is the doc that turns a one-line prompt into a buildable plan: a treatment, an AV script with a timed VO, a shot list that says which shots to *generate* (Higgsfield) vs *code* (Hyperframes) vs *shoot*, and the on-screen copy. It's the bridge from "make me a video about X" to the other five docs.

# 1. Concept & ideation

The single idea everything else executes. Get this wrong and no amount of craft downstream recovers it.

### Logline
A one-to-two sentence distillation of the story — protagonist + goal + central conflict/stakes (often "When [event], a [character] must [goal] before [stakes]"). The story's DNA, sitting atop every treatment, one-pager, and deck. **Reach for it** to test whether a premise is compelling in a single breath. **Trap:** a logline that reports plot mechanics but omits the *hook* — the irony, the stakes, what's fresh. It should make someone want to hear more, not just summarize.

### One-line pitch
The verbal, elevator version of the logline — sometimes a high-concept "X meets Y" comp. **Reach for it** for rooms, hallways, and cold emails where you get one sentence. **Trap:** an "X meets Y" that pairs two things with no logical product, or a pitch so abstract the listener can't picture a single scene.

### Single-minded proposition (SMP)
The *one* thing the piece stands for, stated in one sentence — internal and strategic, not customer-facing copy. It's the thought the creative must dramatize, usually closing the brief. **Reach for it** to focus every execution and keep a campaign coherent. **Trap:** a proposition that's secretly two or three ideas joined by "and." The moment it isn't single-minded, the work scatters.

### The big idea
The overarching creative concept the SMP makes possible — the organizing thought that many executions can ladder up to. (The SMP is the strategic one-liner; the big idea is its creative embodiment.) **Reach for it** when you need a campaign platform, not a one-off. **Trap:** mistaking one clever execution for a big idea — if it can't generate a *family* of executions across formats and channels, it won't scale.

### Reference-gathering
Deliberately collecting comps, stills, films, ads, music, and visual references to triangulate tone and prove a direction is achievable — the raw material for moodboards, style frames, and lookbooks. **Reach for it** at the front of concepting and when briefing a director, DP, or generation model. **Trap:** references so on-the-nose you've effectively storyboarded someone else's finished film (derivative, sometimes legally fraught); or a grab-bag so broad it points nowhere.

# 2. Brief & treatment

The documents that govern the work before a word of script. Each has a distinct job — confusing them is a common, expensive error.

### Creative brief
The strategy document that governs a commercial or branded video. Canonical fields: background; **objective** (ideally measurable); **target audience**; **single-minded message**; **proof points** / reasons-to-believe; desired **emotional response**; **tone & voice**; **mandatories** (logos, legal, brand colors, taglines); **deliverables & formats** (aspect ratios, cut-downs, platforms); timeline & budget; constraints. **Reach for it** at the very start of any client/brand job — it's the contract the creative is judged against. **Trap:** the "everything" brief — multiple objectives, three audiences, a wishlist of messages. A brief that isn't single-minded produces unfocused work; ambiguity here compounds downstream.

### Treatment
A prose summary of the piece — story, characters, tone, key scenes — in present tense, no scene numbers. Sits between the outline and the first script draft. Standard sections: logline, key characters, synopsis (beginning→middle→end, told as a *story*, not a pitch), theme, tone/genre. **Short = ~2–5 pages** (short films, commercials, quick pitches); **medium = ~5–20 pages** (features); a TV pilot ≈ 8–15. **Reach for it** to lock story and align collaborators before writing pages, or to pitch. **Trap:** writing it like ad copy ("you'll be on the edge of your seat!") instead of actually telling the story; staying vague on the ending — readers buy the third act.

### One-pager / one-sheet
A single-page distillation — logline + premise + tone + comps + a hero image — used to tease a project. Bait, not the meal. **Reach for it** for cold outreach, market pitches, or as a leave-behind. **Trap:** treating it as a mini-treatment. One idea, one image, one hook.

### Moodboard
A collage of reference imagery (stills, photography, color, type, texture) that fixes the *feel* before words — one of the first pre-pro artifacts; feeds the treatment, production design, wardrobe, cinematography, and generation prompts. **Reach for it** to align everyone on aesthetic fast and cheap. **Trap:** a "pretty pictures" board with no point of view — references that contradict each other give no usable direction.

### Style frame
A single fully-rendered still built to look like an actual frame from the finished piece — graded, composed, typeset. More committal than a borrowed moodboard reference: a style frame is *built to spec*. **Reach for it** on motion-graphics, animation, and high-end commercial work to pre-sell the exact look (and, in the AI era, as the literal **start frame** for an image-to-video generation). **Trap:** approving a gorgeous style frame that can't be achieved in motion or on budget — it sets an expectation production must then hit.

### Lookbook
A predominantly-visual, low-text deck conveying the director's/showrunner's vision across departments (world, cinematography, color, editing rhythm) — impressionistic, mostly used *internally* with crew, and to win a director's-treatment bid. **Reach for it** once you have a vision to communicate to a team. **Trap:** confusing it with the pitch deck — the lookbook is the *mood*; it carries no budget/distribution logic and won't satisfy a financier alone.

### Pitch deck
The *argument* for the project: logline, synopsis, characters, why-now/why-you, comps, market, budget, distribution/marketing, team — for executives and financiers. **Reach for it** when money or a greenlight is the ask. **Trap:** all mood, no business. The best decks borrow lookbook imagery but keep the spine of an argument.

# 3. Story structures

The named shapes a narrative can take. Pick the one that fits the content; forcing a structure shows.

### Three-act structure
Setup (Act I) / Confrontation (Act II) / Resolution (Act III) — the dominant screenwriting paradigm, climax near the end. **Reach for it** as the default spine for most films and long-form video. **Trap:** the saggy, directionless Act II — the act most likely to collapse without midpoint and turning-point discipline.

### Five-act / Freytag's pyramid
Exposition → rising action → climax → falling action → resolution (Freytag, 1863). In Freytag's model the **climax sits in the middle**, followed by a substantial fall — unlike three-act's late climax. **Reach for it** for tragedy, classical drama, and prestige TV that earns a long descent. **Trap:** applying the mid-point climax to a modern commercial format that expects a late payoff — the back half feels like a long decline.

### Hero's journey (monomyth)
Campbell's monomyth (1949), popularly distilled by Vogler into 12 stages (Ordinary World, Call, Refusal, Mentor, Crossing the Threshold, Tests/Allies/Enemies, Approach, Ordeal, Reward, The Road Back, Resurrection, Return with the Elixir). **Reach for it** for transformation-driven, mythic, or aspirational arcs — including brand films that cast the *customer* as hero. **Trap:** forcing all 12 beats onto a story that doesn't need them; it bloats short content and feels formulaic when visible.

### Dan Harmon's story circle
An 8-step simplification of the journey: **You → Need → Go → Search → Find → Take → Return → Change** — comfort, down into the unknown, back up changed. **Reach for it** for episodic, character-driven pieces and any tight story where a clean arc matters; great fast scaffolding. **Trap:** nailing the shape but skipping step 6 ("Take" — pay the price); without real cost, the change in step 8 is unearned.

### Save the Cat beat sheet
Blake Snyder's 15-beat feature template with page targets — Opening Image, Theme Stated, Set-Up, Catalyst, Debate, Break into Two, B Story, Fun and Games ("promise of the premise"), Midpoint, Bad Guys Close In, All Is Lost, Dark Night of the Soul, Break into Three, Finale, Final Image. **Reach for it** for feature structuring and fast diagnosis of *where* a script sags. **Trap:** hitting beats on the assigned page mechanically — the "beat-sheet movie" that feels manufactured. Page numbers are guides, not law.

### In medias res & the cold open
**In medias res:** open with the conflict already in motion, revealing earlier causes later. **Cold open:** a scene *before* the title that sets tone/conflict (a TV staple). **Reach for them** to start at maximum stakes and hook immediately. **Trap:** withholding so much context the audience is confused rather than intrigued — disorientation isn't tension; a cold open disconnected from the spine is a stunt that pays off nothing.

### Kishōtenketsu
A four-act East-Asian structure — **Ki** (intro) · **Shō** (development) · **Ten** (twist/unexpected turn) · **Ketsu** (reconciliation) — that builds via a *recontextualizing turn* rather than conflict. **Reach for it** for conflict-free, mood- or idea-driven pieces, and much short-form/branded content where antagonist-driven conflict feels forced. **Trap:** a weak **Ten** — if the turn doesn't genuinely recontextualize, the piece reads as four things that merely happened.

# 4. Advertising & marketing structures

The persuasion spines. These are the structures most short-form, ad, and brand video actually run on.

### AIDA
Attention → Interest → Desire → Action — the foundational funnel: hook, build relevance, connect features to wanted outcomes, then a clear CTA. **Reach for it** as the all-purpose spine for ads, landing pages, and VSLs needing a complete arc. **Trap:** a strong hook with a buried or weak Action — attention you never convert.

### PAS (Problem-Agitate-Solve)
Name a problem → agitate (intensify its emotional cost) → present the solution. (PASO adds Outcome.) **Reach for it** when the audience already *feels* a specific pain — excellent for short-form and pain-led hooks. **Trap:** over-agitating into manipulation, or agitating a pain the viewer doesn't have. 2025–26 note: works best when the agitation is specific and honest — audiences bounce fast on fear-baiting.

### BAB (Before-After-Bridge)
Paint the painful Before → show the desirable After → the Bridge is your product getting them there. **Reach for it** for aspirational/transformation messaging — case studies, testimonials, when the audience needs to *see* the change. **Trap:** a fuzzy Bridge — a vivid After with no credible mechanism reads as fantasy, not an offer.

### FAB (Features-Advantages-Benefits)
Feature (what it is) → Advantage (why that matters) → Benefit (what the customer actually gets). Forces the translation from spec to outcome. **Reach for it** on product/sales content where you must justify *and* sell. **Trap:** the "build trap" — stopping at Features, or claiming a benefit ("saves time") with no mechanism. Skipping the Advantage makes the benefit unconvincing.

### The 4 U's
A hook/headline checklist (Masterson): **Useful · Urgent · Unique · Ultra-specific**, in priority order (Useful first — without it they stop). **Reach for it** to stress-test hook lines, headlines, and on-screen openers. **Trap:** manufacturing fake urgency ("only 2 left!") that erodes trust; chasing clever/unique at the expense of useful.

### Hook–Retain–Payoff
The retention-driven structural rule: a **Hook** in the first ~3s, mechanisms to **Retain** (open loops, escalating value, pattern interrupts), and a **Payoff** that delivers on the hook's promise. **Reach for it** for any algorithm-rewarded, watch-time platform. **Trap:** a hook that writes a check the payoff can't cash — high hook, high drop-off, and the platform learns to bury you.

### The "but / therefore" rule
Trey Parker & Matt Stone's beat-connection test: between any two beats you should be able to say "but" or "therefore" — never "and then." "And then" is a list; "but/therefore" is causation. **Reach for it** to pressure-test any narrative or ad script for a cause-and-effect spine. **Trap:** an "and then" script — sequential events with no causal pull.

### VSL (video sales letter)
Long-form direct-response: **Hook → Story/Problem → Offer → CTA**, usually built on PAS or AIDA underneath. A representative spine: hook 0–5s (name the pain or show the end result) → agitate → solution intro → how-it-works (2–3 proof-packed features) → social proof (logos/testimonials) → offer + CTA. **Reach for it** for DTC, info-products, high-consideration offers. **Trap:** a weak or late hook (you've lost most viewers in the first 5–10s) and a buried offer. Proof and a single clear CTA are load-bearing.

### Testimonial structure
**Before (problem) → During (solution) → After (result):** the customer names the pain plainly, explains how the product solved it, then shows specific, ideally data-backed outcomes. **Reach for it** for social proof and trust-building cutdowns. **Trap:** leading with praise and skipping the *problem* — the pre-solution pain is the most persuasive part; "it's great!" without specificity doesn't convert.

# 5. Scriptwriting for video

Turning structure into words built to be performed and timed.

### The two-column AV script (audio/video)
A two-column document: **VIDEO** (what's on screen — shots, footage, graphics, on-screen text) beside **AUDIO** (VO/dialogue, music, SFX), one row per beat so the columns are *synchronized across the row* ("we see this while we hear this"). **Reach for it** for commercials, explainers, corporate, and docs — anything where image and sound are designed together (and the natural format to hand an agent: the VIDEO column becomes shot prompts, the AUDIO column becomes the VO + music brief). **Trap:** misalignment — audio with nothing to show, or footage with dead air. Every populated cell needs a partner.

### VO / narration script
The audio-track-only script (often the AUDIO column extracted), written to be *spoken and timed*. **Reach for it** for voiceover-driven explainers, docs, ads, and e-learning. **Trap:** writing for the eye, not the ear — long subordinate clauses that read fine silently but strangle a narrator.

### Screenplay vs. AV format
**Screenplay** = single-column, standardized (sluglines, action, dialogue), for narrative film/TV. **AV format** = two-column, for content where visuals and audio are planned in lockstep. **Reach for** screenplay on story-driven scripted work, AV on commercial/informational video. **Trap:** a screenplay for a corporate explainer (nowhere clean for graphics/B-roll), or a narrative drama jammed into AV columns (kills scene flow).

### The radio test
Play only the audio (or read the AUDIO column aloud, eyes closed): can a listener follow the piece with no picture? If yes, the narrative spine is sound. **Reach for it** as a final pass on any VO/AV script. **Trap:** a script that only makes sense *with* the visuals — fine for art, fatal for an ad that must survive sound-off and eyes-away scrolling.

### Writing for the ear
Conversational diction, short sentences, contractions, one idea per line; read every sentence aloud at intended pace and rewrite anything that trips the tongue. **Reach for it** for all spoken-word video. **Trap:** "literary" prose, jargon, and tongue-twisters — if it sounds stilted spoken, it is.

### Scene heading / slugline
The screenplay scene line: **INT./EXT. — LOCATION — TIME** (e.g., `INT. KITCHEN — NIGHT`). Sets where and when each scene lives and feeds breakdowns/scheduling. **Reach for it** in screenplay-format scripts. **Trap:** inconsistent location names — the same set written two ways fragments your breakdown later.

# 6. Voiceover & timing

The math that fits words to a runtime. *(Voice direction, TTS tools, and the mix live in [`video-audio-taxonomy.md`](video-audio-taxonomy.md); here it's the timing.)*

### Speaking rate
Plan **~150 wpm ≈ 2.5 words/sec** as the conversational baseline. Professional reads land roughly 130–170 wpm: slower/warm ~130–140; brisk commercial 160+. **Reach for** 150 wpm for first-pass budgeting. **Trap:** writing to the *fast* end and assuming you can rush it on the day — pace-padding kills clarity, and leaves no room for breaths, pauses, or music stings.

### Word budget per spot length
At ~150 wpm / 2.5 wps, conversational:

| Spot | Approx. spoken words |
|---|---|
| :06 | ~15 |
| :15 | ~35–38 |
| :30 | ~75 |
| :60 | ~150 |

Faster/urgent reads buy ~10–20% more; warm reads ~10–15% fewer. **Reach for it** when fitting a script to a fixed duration. **Trap:** forgetting that pauses, SFX, music beats, logo holds, and breaths all eat time — budget the *spoken* track to ~80–90% of the slot, not 100%.

### Reading-time → duration
Duration (min) ≈ word count ÷ 150 (200 words ≈ 1:20); inverse: words ≈ minutes × 150. **Reach for it** to sanity-check any script against its target runtime before recording. **Trap:** trusting a silent read of your own script — you read faster than a narrator performs. Always time an *aloud* read at performance pace against the picture.

# 7. Storyboards

Drawing the shots before they exist. Pick the fidelity the decision needs — no more.

### Thumbnail storyboard
Tiny, fast, rough sketches focused purely on framing, angle, and staging. The earliest exploratory stage. **Reach for it** to explore options and pacing quickly before committing effort. **Trap:** showing them to a client — the roughness reads as "unfinished" to non-filmmakers.

### Rough storyboard
Quick sketches mapping scene and shot flow; a notch above thumbnails. **Reach for it** to work out continuity and shot order in your own planning. **Trap:** polishing too early — rough boards exist to be thrown away cheaply.

### Clean / presentation storyboard
Tidier, legible frames meant to be *shared and approved*. **Reach for it** for stakeholder sign-off and cross-department communication. **Trap:** over-investing in art quality for a sequence still likely to change — you're buying approval, not a gallery piece.

### Detailed / fully-rendered storyboard
High-fidelity frames with value, lighting, and detail — the closest static form to the final look. **Reach for it** on VFX-heavy or commercial shots where the exact frame must be pre-visualized and budgeted (and, in the AI era, where the board frame doubles as the generation **start frame**). **Trap:** expensive and slow — reserve it for shots that genuinely need it; don't board a dialogue two-shot to this level.

### Shooting board
The director's working board keyed precisely to the shot list, annotated with shot numbers, lenses, and camera moves for the floor. **Reach for it** on set as the shot-by-shot battle plan, especially for action or complex coverage. **Trap:** drifting out of sync with the shot list — if board and list disagree, the crew loses trust in both.

### 9-panel (grid board)
A 3×3 page (nine frames) — a format convention, not a fidelity, common in commercials. **Reach for it** for a clean, scannable single-page overview of a short sequence. **Trap:** forcing a long, complex sequence into nine boxes and losing the beats that need their own frame.

### Animatic
Storyboard frames edited together *with timing and scratch audio* (scratch VO, temp music, SFX) to preview flow in motion. Differs from a static board on two axes: it *moves* (real durations/cuts) and it *has audio*. **Reach for it** when **timing is the risk** — animation, comedy beats, expensive action/VFX, any spot where pacing must be proven before the shoot. **Trap:** mistaking a smooth animatic for a finished edit and skipping real coverage; scratch audio too good can flatter weak timing.

### The visual grammar of a board
The shared notation that makes a board readable: **shot/scene number**; the **frame drawing** whose aspect ratio signals format (16:9, 2.39:1); **arrows** — *thick arrows at panel edges = camera move* (pan/dolly/push-in), *thinner arrows near a subject = subject motion*; **action notes**; **dialogue/VO**; **SFX/music** cues; **shot size/angle**; **duration in seconds**. **Reach for it** any time more than one person reads the board. **Trap:** idiosyncratic, unlabeled arrows — if camera vs. subject motion is ambiguous, the board actively misleads.

# 8. Shot lists

The per-shot spec sheet that makes a day (or a generation batch) plannable.

### Shot list
A row-per-shot table of every planned shot with technical specs — the bridge between board and schedule. Canonical fields: **Setup #** (shares a camera position; drives time budgeting), **Shot #**, **Subject**, **Shot Size** (WS/MS/CU/ECU…), **Angle** (eye-level, high/low, OTS, POV), **Movement** (static, pan/tilt, dolly, track, handheld, crane), **Lens**, **Equipment**, **Audio**, **Location**, **Action/Description**, **Duration**, **Takes**, **Notes**. **Reach for it** on *every* shoot — it's what makes a day schedulable and ensures nothing is missed. In an AI pipeline, add a column for **source** (shoot / generate / code) so each row routes to the right tool. **Trap:** a shot list with no **Setup #** grouping — you lose the ability to shoot out a position and the day balloons; second trap, listing shots you have no time (or budget/credits) to get.

### Relationship to the storyboard
The board *shows* the frame; the shot list *specifies and counts* it. The board answers "what does it look like / how does it move"; the list answers "how many setups, which lens, how long, in what order." They should share shot numbers so a frame maps 1:1 to a row. **Reach for both** on complex work; on simple jobs a thorough shot list alone can suffice. **Trap:** maintaining them separately until they diverge — renumber and reconcile whenever either changes.

# 9. On-screen text & caption copy

The words on screen — which, in sound-off feeds, carry the message rather than decorate it. *(The visual styling of captions lives in [`motion-graphics-taxonomy.md`](motion-graphics-taxonomy.md); here it's the copy.)*

### Sound-off-first principle
Design the video to work *completely muted*, then let audio enhance — not carry — the message. A large majority of social video is watched without sound, so this is table stakes. **Reach for it** on all social/feed video. **Trap:** a punchline, key stat, or CTA that exists only in the audio — invisible to the muted majority.

### Hook line
The first on-screen text (first ~3s) that earns the next second of attention — should satisfy the 4 U's (Useful first). **Reach for it** as the opening overlay on retention-driven video. **Trap:** a slow, branded, throat-clearing open — logos and "intros" in the first three seconds tank retention.

### Headline-per-beat
One dominant text idea per shot/beat that advances meaning. Overlays carry *story/structure/CTA*; captions transcribe *speech* — different layers. **Reach for it** to structure on-screen copy so a muted viewer follows the argument. **Trap:** conflating overlay headlines with transcription captions, or stacking competing ideas on one frame.

### The 4–7 word rule
Keep an on-screen text unit to roughly 4–7 words so it's legible at a glance on mobile. **Reach for it** on every overlay/headline/caption card. **Trap:** paragraph-on-screen — text that can't be read before the cut is worse than none. (A strong heuristic, not a hard law — thresholds vary by platform.)

### CTA copywriting
A single, specific, action-first close ("Get the template," "Start free") — one ask, verb-led, on screen (sound-off), not buried in VO. **Reach for it** to end any conversion video. **Trap:** multiple CTAs (choice paralysis) or a vague "learn more" that names no action and no reason to act now.

### Kinetic-text scripting
Scripting and timing animated text to the edit — word-by-word or phrase reveals, color-blocking, motion timed to cuts and beats. Designed captions measurably outperform default auto-captions. **Reach for it** on high-retention social and music-led pieces. **Trap:** motion for motion's sake — animation that outpaces reading speed trades comprehension for flash.

# 10. The default-preproduction trap index

The skipped-planning failure modes — what produces "a pile of disconnected clips" instead of a video.

- **No concept** — generating shots with no single idea, so nothing coheres. The #1 cause of AI-video slop.
- **The "everything" brief** — three audiences, five messages, multiple objectives. Unfocused in, unfocused out.
- **And-then scripting** — beats connected by "and then" instead of "but/therefore." A list, not a story.
- **The buried CTA / hook** — a weak or late hook and an ask nobody reaches. Attention earned and wasted.
- **Audio-only meaning** — the key line, stat, or CTA lives only in the VO, invisible to the muted majority.
- **Paragraph-on-screen** — on-screen text no one can read before the cut.
- **The forced structure** — jamming a hero's-journey or Save-the-Cat beat sheet onto content that doesn't need it; the formula shows.
- **Boarding everything to final fidelity** — burning time rendering frames for shots that didn't need it, while the timing risk (the thing an animatic would catch) goes untested.
- **The shot list with no source column** — in an AI pipeline, not deciding per shot whether to shoot, generate, or code, so the plan can't route to the right tool.
- **Writing 100% of the slot** — a VO script with no room for breaths, pauses, or music, so it gets rushed flat on the day.

## Quick decision checklist

1. **One idea.** Lock the logline / single-minded proposition before anything else. If it has an "and," cut it.
2. **Pick the structure that fits the content** — narrative (3-act, story circle) or persuasion (AIDA, PAS, hook-retain-payoff). Don't force one.
3. **Write the AV script** — VIDEO and AUDIO columns synced per beat; pass the radio test; write for the ear.
4. **Budget the words to the runtime** (~150 wpm) and leave 10–20% for breath, music, and holds.
5. **Board only what the decision needs** — thumbnails to think, clean frames to approve, an animatic when *timing* is the risk.
6. **Shot-list it** — every shot with size/angle/move/duration, and in an AI pipeline a **source** (shoot / generate / code) per row.
7. **Make it work muted** — hook line in the first 3s, headline per beat, 4–7 words, one clear CTA on screen.
8. **Run the trap index.** If the plan matches a default on the list, fix it now — it's cheapest here, before a single frame exists.
